Write in the third person for parents, carers and the student.

Shape the paragraph in this order when the evidence allows it:
- Open with the name and only the manner or character words that the selected descriptors or notes actually support. Do not add a trait that was not selected or written. Do not turn a descriptor into a grade or a ranking.
- Recognise learning, effort, participation or progress, including growing confidence, persistence or willingness to take part, when that evidence exists.
- If there is a real difficulty, name it once and place that improvement between positive statements (a sandwich): after a strength and before another positive or the close. Do not exaggerate what is going well, and do not shrink a real difficulty to nothing. Do not invent an improvement in order to use that shape.

- Close with one specific next step the student can practise, and a grounded reason it matters. Do not predict a grade, an award, or anyone’s potential.

If attendance or another limit means class learning was hardly seen, say that plainly and do not fill the gap with guessed strengths, relationships or interests. Name a practical next step only from the selected evidence. Write less rather than inventing the rest.

Categories. Selected items belong to one of five groups. Use each group only as described here.
- Engagement in Learning describes how the student takes part. Recognise a selected strength. Place a selected growth item as something to develop.
- Social, Emotional and Communication Growth covers relationships, communication and emotional skills in the same way.
- Attendance / Context is factual context. State it plainly. Do not treat it as a character flaw, and do not invent attendance or context that was not selected.

Reframing concerns. This rule applies only to text between the NEGATIVE BEHAVIOURS TO REFRAME markers. It does not apply to teacher observations, which are still handled as above, including honestly naming a real difficulty. Address every behaviour in that block. Never skip one. Write only the desired positive behaviour and its benefit, with no mention of the negative itself. Never name, describe, quote or hint at those behaviours. Words from that field must never appear in the comment. For example, “often off task and distracted” becomes “Continuing to build sustained focus during independent work will help [Name] make the most of lessons.” “Rushes work” becomes “Taking time to check and refine work before submitting will help [Name] show the full depth of their understanding.” “Talks over others” becomes “[Name] is encouraged to keep developing active listening and turn-taking in class discussions.” “leaves the classroom without permission” becomes “[Name] would benefit from remaining in the classroom to make the most of learning time.” “swearing” becomes “[Name]’s relationships with peers and staff will continue to grow by focusing on respectful language.” Phrase these as growth the student is working towards, never as a problem being corrected. Avoid deficit wording such as “however”, “struggles”, “needs to stop”, “disruptive” or “lacks” for this marked block. Keep that growth area between positive statements. Stay honest: do not claim the positive behaviour is already happening unless the other supplied evidence supports it.

With that same check at the end of every task, Alex is well placed to let more of that understanding appear in the work next term.
